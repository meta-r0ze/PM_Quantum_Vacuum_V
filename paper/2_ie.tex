\section{What It Means for a System to Decay}

Any system that persists must process state transitions within finite hardware capacity. When its internal complexity or transition frequency exceeds this threshold, the system faces a Ledger Crisis: it can no longer balance continuous dissipation against discrete allocations without erasing information. This section formalizes the universal mechanics of how systems degrade, decay, or transition under this constraint.

\subsection{The Thermodynamic Distinction: Mass vs. Drag}

In Informational Energetics, \emph{mass} is the thermodynamic latency of a routing protocol---the static structural cost of maintaining a specific geometric configuration. \emph{Drag} is the ongoing toll: the continuous dissipation incurred as the system processes state transitions at frequency $\lambda$. 

A system may possess high mass yet remain stable if its protocol is a Topological Ground State (no simpler route exists). Its drag is bounded and sustainable. Conversely, an overtone protocol---a higher-dimensional resonance of the same base structure---carries excess geometric residual that generates friction against the substrate. Its drag scales with both its structural complexity $\mathbf{K}$ and its processing frequency:
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{\text{drag}} = \xi \cdot \mathbf{K} \cdot \lambda
\end{equation}
where $\xi \ge k_B T \ln 2$ is the Landauer cost per state transition. When $\Phi_{\text{drag}}$ exceeds the local hardware capacity $CAP$, the system hits insolvency.

\subsection{The Ledger Crisis: Insolvency as Geometric Pathology}

On a discrete substrate, insolvency is not a gradual decline but a specific digital pathology. Each node possesses finite channel capacity---on the $E_8$ substrate, $\nu = 16$ active chiral channels per clock cycle. When a protocol's state transitions demand concurrent writes that oversubscribe these channels, distinct causal histories collide in the same temporal slot. The node lacks residual bandwidth to resolve the ambiguity. This is \emph{Causal Aliasing}: the collapse of distinguishable states into an indistinguishable superposition, resulting in immediate information erasure.

The Ledger Crisis is therefore a hardware collision. The system cannot ``borrow'' capacity; it cannot defer state updates. It must either shed complexity or dissolve into the vacuum noise floor.

\subsection{Structural Shedding: Decay as Deterministic Re-Routing}

To survive a Ledger Crisis, the Principle of Least Entropic Action forces the system to undergo Structural Shedding. The high-latency payload is fragmented and rerouted into lower-complexity protocols that fit within available channel capacity. This is not stochastic collapse; it is deterministic network re-routing governed by two strict bounds:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Interface Aperture ($\mathcal{A}$):} The geometric bandwidth of the communication link through which complexity must exit. A narrow aperture (e.g., the 1D chiral W-boson channel) bottlenecks the decay; a wide aperture (e.g., the 3D color bulk) permits rapid dissipation.
    \item \textbf{Dissipation Volume ($\mathcal{V}$):} The available phase space of the lower-tier destination---the channel capacity and temporal bandwidth of the simpler protocol receiving the shed complexity.
\end{itemize}

The decay rate is strictly bounded by the slower of these two constraints:
\begin{equation}
\Gamma \propto \mathcal{A}^2 \cdot \mathcal{V}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Stability: The Topological Lock}

A state is stable if, and only if, it is a \emph{Topological Ground State}: no simpler protocol exists that can route its fundamental payload without violating the system's core boundary constraints ($\text{Inside} \neq \text{Outside}$). 

The electron is the ground-state singlet protocol; no simpler route preserves its $Q = -1$ boundary flux. The proton is the ground-state fractional protocol; no simpler configuration closes the 3D color bulk. Because no lower-tier destination exists for their respective payloads, they are topologically locked---even under Ledger Crisis, the substrate has no valid re-routing target. They persist indefinitely.

Instability arises only when a lower-latency protocol exists within the same topological class. The muon decays to the electron because both are $Q = -1$ boundary-coupled singlets; the muon is simply a higher-dimensional overtone with excess geometric residual that generates unsustainable drag.

\subsection{The Illusion of Stochasticity}

If decay is a deterministic re-routing event triggered by Ledger Crisis, why does it appear fundamentally probabilistic---yielding a half-life---to macroscopic observers?

The substrate is an active, dynamic network. A heavy protocol sits exactly at the threshold of insolvency. However, the exact moment the local capacity overflows depends on the ambient noise floor of the vacuum. Background network traffic---vacuum fluctuations---constantly washes over the localized state. Decay occurs when a specific local geometric fluctuation temporarily pushes the node's capacity over its strict finite limit, triggering the deterministic re-routing algorithm. Because these vacuum fluctuations are pseudo-randomly distributed across the macroscopic lattice, the exact arrival time of the fatal capacity spike follows a Poisson distribution. 

Thus, decay is strictly deterministic at the substrate scale, but emerges as a statistical probability---a half-life---at the macroscopic level, perfectly analogous to packet timeout probabilities in congested digital networks.
